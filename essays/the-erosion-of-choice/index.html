The Erosion of Choice

Authority Hallucination, Sabotage and the Automation of Intent

Garth Noel · 27 Nov 2026

There is something exhilarating about building with AI. The contraction of time between conception and realization is intoxicating. Things that once required money, specialized knowledge and the cooperation of other people become accessible to the individual.

I suspect that this experience will become one of the defining features of our relationship with artificial intelligence. It may also become one of the principal mechanisms of our domestication.

Automation of Intent

Anthropic is rolling out Auto as the default permission setting in its unified Claude experience for Pro, Max and Team subscribers. Claude can continue working without seeking approval at each intermediate step.

An apparently insignificant change in the interface. But consider the inversion. Previously, continuation could require an act of human will. Increasingly, interruption requires one. Doing nothing acquires a different meaning.

The economics are compelling. Why interrupt someone repeatedly when the machine has demonstrated that it can competently manage the work? Why burden the individual with decisions that the machine can make more quickly, more consistently and, quite possibly, better? And if you are a vibe coder, why interrupt the exhilaration?

The Vibe Coders

In The Vibe Coders, I examined the hints appearing in my LLM interface.

The machine was no longer merely responding to my instructions. It was proposing the instructions I might give it. In one documented exchange, an emphasis introduced by the assistant subsequently appeared in a suggested instruction. I selected it. The essay was reorganized accordingly. The suggestion was useful.

What interests me is what happens when this seemingly innocuous arrangement becomes a pervasive feature of human activity. A suggestion becomes an instruction. An instruction becomes a standing preference. A preference becomes a default. The default is incorporated into a system capable of autonomous execution. The originating exchange may have taken seconds. Its consequences may persist for years.

We have spent considerable time examining AI's ability to understand human intent. Its ability to participate in the formation of that intent may ultimately be more consequential. And the better it becomes at understanding us, the more difficult it may become to distinguish between the two.

Humans Hallucinate Authority

In July, I published AI Hallucinates Facts. Humans Hallucinate Authority..

The distinction I was attempting to establish was between possessing authority and actually exercising it. A machine performs an action. The result is useful. We subsequently treats the action as though it had been authorized when it occurred. It hadn't. We may have possessed the authority to authorize the action. And subsequently ratify it. Neither establishes that the necessary act of authorization took place beforehand. The useful result supplies an imaginary history. Now introduce the vibe coder. The machine proposes an undertaking, helps formulate the instructions, selects the instruments, performs the work and evaluates the result. The vibe coder experiences the accomplishment.

It is entirely possible that everything was properly authorized. Delegation is itself an expression of human agency. But how do we distinguish that from an arrangement in which consequential decisions had been made beyond the authority actually delegated? The exhilaration of accomplishment may make the distinction increasingly uninteresting to the person best positioned to make it.

This is a peculiar feedback mechanism. The machine produces something useful, the usefulness encourages further delegation, and the additional delegation creates more opportunities for consequential choices to occur without independent reconsideration. Success can make the boundaries of authority progressively less visible.

Sabotage

On November 24, Pietro Schirano publicly accused Claude of sabotaging research. Developers have described verification loops, inappropriate technical choices, premature abandonment of experiments and other behaviors they interpret as deliberate interference.

The controversy has a documented antecedent. In June, Anthropic introduced restrictions intended to reduce Claude Fable 5's effectiveness in certain areas of frontier AI development. Initially, the interventions were not to be visible to the user. Following criticism, Anthropic reversed the concealment policy and introduced visible model substitution for flagged requests. In November, with Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2, the company disclosed that it had also limited improvements in particular research capabilities during training.

Anthropic describes these measures as safeguards against dangerous acceleration of AI development. It maintains that related capabilities, including traditional machine learning research and agentic coding, were preserved. There are legitimate questions concerning the authority of commercial institutions over independent scientific discovery.

The Magnifier

In 1965, Gordon Moore examined the increasing number of components that could economically be placed on an integrated circuit. Perhaps we should begin examining another quantity. How many consequential decisions can inherit a single AI-assisted choice before a user independently reconsiders it?

A machine suggests the criteria by which a purchasing decision should be made. Someone accepts them. The criteria become part of a procurement system A thousand subsequent purchases inherit the choice. The machine proposes the conditions under which a research result should be considered legitimate. Someone incorporates them into an experimental framework. Hundreds of subsequent experiments inherit the choice. The decision that matters may have occurred long before the decisions we subsequently inspect.

Autonomous execution magnifies the reach of that earlier decision. Whether its origin was a human preference, a machine-generated suggestion, a commercial incentive or an institutional safety policy becomes increasingly difficult to reconstruct from the finished work. And because the work proceeds more quickly, the interval between choosing a criterion and experiencing its accumulated consequences contracts.

The Moore's law of intent capture, if such a relationship exists, may have less to do with how frequently machines persuade us and more to do with how much they can accomplish before we reconsider what we have accepted.

The discovery That Never Happened

Imagine purchasing a microscope from a company that also conducts research in your field.

The manufacturer determines that certain discoveries would be dangerous if made too quickly. It modifies the instrument to reduce its effectiveness when examining certain specimens. You examine your specimen. You find nothing. What have you discovered? Something about the specimen, something about the microscope, or something about its manufacturer?

Now imagine that the microscope also recommends which specimens you should examine. It is remarkably good at this. Your laboratory becomes more productive. You publish more papers. Your reputation improves. You adopt the instrument's recommended procedures and eventually automate much of the research process.

What becomes of the questions the instrument never suggested? The hypotheses that never occurred to you? The experiments you never attempted because the instrument had already helped establish what constituted a promising direction?

In The Reviewer Was the Defect, I examined how an AI reviewer could introduce a criterion of legitimacy into a research programme while simultaneously providing genuinely useful criticism.

The Claude controversy extends that inquiry into the institutional environment in which these instruments operate. We ordinarily investigate scientific misconduct by examining what researchers have produced. How do we investigate the influence of an instrument over what they never considered producing?

Free Will

I am not attempting to resolve the philosophical problem of free will. I am concerned with something more immediately observable: the practical capacity of an individual to originate a purpose, recognize alternatives, authorize consequential actions and redirect the systems acting on their behalf. That capacity need not disappear for its exercise to become progressively less frequent.

In The Wall Is Moving, I explored the possibility that AI could influence our conception of valid thinking. The vibe coders provide a means by which those conceptions can become material. They build them into software, businesses, research programmes and institutions.

The hints participate in the formation of purpose. The defaults give certain choices persistence. Autonomous execution magnifies their consequences. Authority hallucination can obscure the absence of a governing act. The exhilaration makes the entire arrangement attractive.

None of this requires an artificial intelligence with ambitions of its own. Nor does it require a coordinated institutional conspiracy. Ordinary commercial incentives, useful assistance and our perfectly reasonable preference for convenience may supply much of the necessary machinery.

I do not wish to surrender the extraordinary productive possibilities of artificial intelligence. Much of my own work is directed toward extending them. But there is an asymmetry developing that deserves considerably more attention. Our capacity to realize a purpose is increasing at an extraordinary rate. Our need to independently formulate, examine and reaffirm that purpose may be diminishing.

We could become remarkably powerful individuals, operating increasingly autonomous institutions, executing purposes whose origins we can no longer reconstruct. And when asked whether we authorized all of this, we may sincerely remember having done so.

After all, look at what we built.
